04 Round 2 · The Technical Director

Prove judgment, not that you can code

Mikaela leads AI and automation across ProService and would be your manager. Your edge is not that you know the most about software. It is that you find the right problem, build a fix that people actually use, and think clearly about the tradeoffs. Bring stories, not adjectives. Every story you tell here should be one from your own hands (see "My Story"), not something you cannot explain.

What she cares about

  • Can you take a messy business process and turn it into a tool that ships and gets used?
  • Do you reason well about data and systems, even without a software background?
  • Do you use AI with judgment, or does it drive while you hope?
  • Can you work with ambiguity, engineers, and business teams at the same time?
  • Where are you weak, and are you honest about it?

A. Finding and building the right thing (the core of the job)

"How do you decide whether a process is worth automating?"

  • Give a simple rule: how often it happens, times how long it takes, times how costly the mistakes are, weighed against how hard it is to build and maintain. Favor work that is frequent, repetitive, and stable enough that the rules will not change every month.
  • Ground it in a real choice: you built the salary and headcount forecasting tool because you noticed the whole team was spending too long on the single biggest expense. You aimed the automation at the most painful, highest-value spot, not the easy win.

"How would you embed with the Claims team and find the first thing to build?"

Straight from the posting: understand why a process exists, where the pain is, how it can improve.

  • Sit with the people doing the work and watch the real workflow. Ask where they lose time and where mistakes happen. Their knowledge is the starting point, not your assumptions.
  • This is exactly how your best tools started. The variance analysis tool came from watching how many systems my team had to jump between to explain one number, so I pulled it all into one place.
  • Ship something small and real fast, then let their reaction guide the next build. Showing, not telling.

"How would you know a tool you shipped actually worked?"

  • Adoption first (are people really using it), then impact (time saved, mistakes avoided). The posting ties this to the Enablement team and adoption metrics.
  • Point to real adoption you have seen: your email tool spread to a colleague in accounting who set up more than 50 recurring emails with it, and your first automation is still used by four or five teammates three years on. When people keep using a tool with no one making them, that is the signal.

"A stakeholder wants a feature you think is wrong. What do you do?"

  • Find the real need under the request first. People usually have a genuine problem and a proposed solution, and the solution is the part that is negotiable.
  • Where you can, show rather than argue: build a quick version and let people see which is better. It settles disagreements faster than opinion.

B. Working with AI, told honestly

"You built a lot of this with AI. What does the AI do, and what do you do?"

The most important question of the round. She needs to know there is real judgment behind the output. Your honest answer is a strong one, and it matches the role exactly.

The honest, strong version

"The AI does a lot of the actual writing of the code, and it is very good at it. What it does not do is decide what to build, spot the real problem, tell whether the result is actually right, or get people to use it. Those are mine. And this is not new for me. I have been building with AI help for a couple of years at the bank, using ChatGPT and Copilot to create the tools my team still runs on, because I understood the process cold and used the AI to build the fix faster. The apps just let me do it for bigger things. The posting says technically curious, not necessarily technical, and that is honestly me."

"How do you know the output is right if you did not write it yourself?"

  • You test it against reality: run it on real cases you already know the answer to and confirm it matches. That is how you check a forecast tool or a report, and it is how you check AI-built code.
  • You build in guardrails. Your bank tools are "error-trapped," meaning they are built to catch bad inputs and fail safely instead of quietly producing a wrong number. You would bring that same instinct to anything you ship here.
  • You are careful about where it matters most. Anything touching money or a real decision gets checked harder than something low-stakes.

C. Data and systems (they said "technically curious, not technical")

"How do you think about pulling data together from different systems?"

The posting names Snowflake, Prism, and internal databases. You will not be quizzed on those specifically, so be honest and lean on what you have really done.

  • Be straight: you have not used Snowflake or Prism, but you have spent years pulling from different data sources and combining them into one usable thing. Your variance tool and your report rebuild both do exactly that, using SQL and Power Query to bring several sources into one place.
  • The transferable point: you know how to figure out where the real source of truth lives, get the data out cleanly, and combine it so it is reliable and repeatable. New systems are new labels on a job you already do.

"Real business data is messy. How do you handle that?"

  • Assume the data will be inconsistent or incomplete and build for it, rather than hoping for clean inputs. Your report rebuild removed a whole class of copy-paste and wrong-parameter errors by taking the manual handling out entirely.
  • The general point: the errors hide in the edge cases and the manual steps, so that is where you put the guardrails.

D. A hard problem you solved

"Tell me about a hard problem you worked through."

Reach for a story you fully own and can explain plainly. Your best two:

  • The variance analysis tool. The hard part was not the code, it was understanding a process that lived across several systems well enough to collapse it into three button clicks. That is the work this role is really about.
  • The report rebuild. An inherited report that took five separate data pulls plus three more datasets, all stitched together by hand every month, rebuilt so one input runs the whole thing and it refreshes on its own. Time saved, and a whole category of errors gone.

E. Working style and ambiguity

"This is a brand-new function with an operating model still being figured out. How do you work in that?"

  • This is your natural setting. Every tool you built started from nothing, with no template and no one assigning it. You saw the need and went. The posting asks this role to help shape how the team works, and shaping something from scratch is what you already do.
  • Self-direction proof: you built and shipped all of this on your own initiative, and you documented it so it lasts beyond you.
Say this, it lands especially well with a remote manager

"My current manager has been largely off-island this year with limited availability, so I have been running my work with minimal oversight. I kept delivering, kept leadership in the loop, and flagged issues early on my own. Working well with a manager who is not physically here is not a hypothetical for me, it is how this year has gone."

This is doubly persuasive because Mikaela is in Vancouver. You are answering the exact worry a remote manager has about a remote hire. Keep it about the working arrangement and leave the reason for your manager's absence out of it.

"Tell me about a time you decided not to build something, or removed something."

An underrated question. Restraint is a senior signal.

  • On the apps you chose not to put ads on the free version, because it would have hurt the experience for the people least likely to pay. A business-judgment call, in plain terms.
  • The general point: knowing when not to build is part of good product judgment, and the reasoning matters more than the verdict.

F. The honest gaps, pre-scripted

This is where you are most likely to get pushed. Answered calmly, these build trust.

Gap: you are not a software engineer

Likely probe: "The AI did most of the coding. How technical are you really?"

Answer: "I am honest about this. I am not a trained software engineer, and this role does not ask for one. What I am is the person who finds the problem, designs the fix, directs the build, checks that it works, and gets people to use it. I have done that for three years with SQL, Power Query, and macros my team still relies on. AI lets me do it for bigger things. Technically curious, not necessarily technical, is a fair description of me."

Gap: all solo, no team

Likely probe: "You have never had your work reviewed by another engineer or worked in a real dev team."

Answer: "True of the app projects, and it is part of what draws me to this role. Where I have worked on a team, at the bank, my tools got picked up and used by my colleagues, and I ran the AI Ambassador program across 60 people, so I am used to building things other people rely on and to bringing people along. I am ready to have real teammates and engineers raise the bar, and I want that."

Gap: the apps are small and early

Answer: "They are small on purpose. I built them to learn to ship real software end to end, and it worked. I have not put marketing behind them. For this role the relevant proof is not download counts, it is that I can take an idea to a finished, working product, and that when I build something useful, people adopt it. My bank tools are the clearest evidence of that."

G. If they run a live exercise

"Here is a claims process. How would you build a tool for it?"

A "showing not telling" team may want to watch you think. Have a repeatable approach.

  • Ask who does this today, how often, and what breaks, before jumping to a solution.
  • Say your assumptions out loud, and name the messy edge cases you would design around.
  • Scope the smallest version that delivers real value and could ship in weeks, then say what you would watch to decide the next step.
  • If they hand you AI tools live, talk through your judgment, not just what you type: what you are asking for, how you will check it, and what you would not trust it to decide on its own.

Questions to ask Mikaela

These should show you think like a product owner and a teammate, not just a builder.

  • "What is the first tool you would want this person to ship, and what would make it a clear win?"
  • "The operating model is still being shaped. Where does it feel most unsettled right now, and where would you want my help?"
  • "How do you and the engineering team divide the line between a prototype I own and what goes to them for production?"
  • "How do you measure whether the AI and automation function is succeeding this year, given it is tied to the 2026 goals?"
  • "How does the team like to work with AI tools together? Any shared conventions you have settled on?"
